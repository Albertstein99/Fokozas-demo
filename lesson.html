A circle, a triangle,
a theorem.

Mathematical diagrams can be experiments. Move a point, watch what changes, and look for what stays the same.

01. A right angle in a circle

The two fixed gray points are the endpoints of a diameter. Drag the purple point around the circle: the angle at that point remains 90°, except at the endpoints where the triangle collapses. This is Thales’ theorem.

Join all three points to the circle’s center. Two isosceles triangles express the angle at the moving point as half the straight angle subtended by the diameter.

∠ACB = 90°

02. Three medians, one center

A median connects a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Move any purple vertex: all three medians still pass through the gray centroid G. It divides each median in a 2:1 ratio, measured from the vertex.

G = (A + B + C) / 3

03. A line through three centers

The circumcenter O, centroid G and orthocenter H lie on the Euler line. Drag the triangle into an obtuse shape: some centers move outside it, while the collinearity remains. For an equilateral triangle the three centers coincide, so they do not determine a unique line.

H = 3G − 2O

These examples recompute their geometry when an input point moves. Experimentation illustrates the theorem; the algebra above explains why the relation persists.
